Your Model Comparison Is Probably Lying to You

How a “p = 0.00000000001” win for LightGBM turned into “just ship the logistic regression” — an A/B test of three classifiers, done properly.

Every data team has had this conversation:

“The new gradient-boosted model scores 94.1% macro-F1 in cross-validation. The old logistic regression gets 93.7%. And the t-test on the folds says p < 0.001. Let’s ship it.”

It sounds rigorous: cross-validation, a statistical test, a tiny p-value. But almost every part of that reasoning is shaky. In this post I run a proper A/B test between three classifiers, show exactly where the naive approach breaks, and end up with a very different recommendation.

All code, data pipeline, tests and the full report are open source: github.com/akhilnathreddy/ml-ab-testing

The setup: three models, one benchmark, rules written in advance

The dataset. The Dry Bean dataset from UCI has 13,611 images of dry beans reduced to 16 shape features (area, perimeter, roundness, …). The task is to classify them into 7 varieties. It’s a good benchmark for this because:

  • It’s multi-class and imbalanced (the largest class is 6.8× the smallest), so macro-F1 matters.
  • It isn’t saturated. Unlike Iris or Wine, the models don’t all score 99%, so there’s something to test.

The three arms of the test:

  • A (control): Logistic Regression, with feature scaling
  • B: Random Forest, 500 trees
  • C: LightGBM, 400 boosted trees

Pre-registration. Before training anything, I wrote down the rules (they’re in the repo’s PLAN.md):

  • Primary metric: macro-F1
  • Significance level: α = 0.05, with Holm correction for the 3 pairwise comparisons
  • Smallest difference worth caring about: 1 percentage point
  • Decision rule: switch from the control only if a challenger is both statistically significant and at least 1 pp better. If the models are provably equivalent, pick the simpler one.

This step is not bureaucracy. Without it, every number you see afterwards invites you to move the goalposts.

One data-hygiene catch: the raw file contains 68 exact duplicate rows, all of them HOROZ beans. If you don’t drop them, the same bean can sit in both a training fold and a test fold, which quietly inflates every score.

Step 1: Cross-validate, and pair the comparison

I used 10×10 repeated stratified cross-validation on 80% of the data, which gives 100 test folds. Crucially, every model sees exactly the same folds. That makes this a paired experiment, like testing two running shoes on the same runner instead of on different runners.

Look at the spread. The difference between folds (sd ≈ 0.7 pp) is bigger than the difference between models (at most 0.4 pp). Comparing averages alone would tell you nothing. But because the folds are shared, we can look at the per-fold difference, which cancels out “this fold was just hard”:

LightGBM beats Logistic Regression on 81 of 100 folds and Random Forest on 79. Random Forest vs Logistic Regression is a coin flip (46 vs 54).

Step 2: The trap — the naive paired t-test

The obvious move is scipy.stats.ttest_rel(lgbm_scores, logreg_scores). Here’s what it says (Holm-corrected):

                    Δ macro-F1   naive p (Holm)
LightGBM − LogReg     +0.43 pp   1.3 × 10⁻¹¹
LightGBM − RF         +0.42 pp   4.0 × 10⁻¹³

Case closed? No. This test is invalid for cross-validation scores. A t-test assumes the 100 numbers are independent. But in 10-fold CV, any two training sets share about 89% of their rows, so the scores are strongly correlated. The test sees 100 data points when it has far less independent information than that. The variance is underestimated, and the p-value is absurdly small.

Dietterich showed back in 1998 that this test’s false-positive rate is far above 5%. Adding more CV repeats makes it worse: you can push p toward zero just by running more folds.

Step 3: Tests that respect the dependence

1. The corrected resampled t-test (Nadeau & Bengio, 2003). It’s the same t-statistic, but the variance is inflated to account for the overlap between training sets:

t = mean(d) / sqrt( (1/J + n_test/n_train) · var(d) )

With J = 100 folds and n_test/n_train ≈ 0.111, the variance multiplier goes from 0.01 to 0.121, so the standard error grows about 3.5×.

2. The 5×2cv t-test (Dietterich, 1998) and Alpaydin’s combined F-test. These use five repeats of 2-fold CV. The two halves never overlap, so the false-positive rate stays honest. The price is lower power.

Same data, very different conclusions:

                    Δ (pp)   95% CI (pp)      p (Holm)
RF − LogReg          +0.01   [−0.32, +0.34]   0.952
LightGBM − LogReg    +0.43   [+0.05, +0.80]   0.055
LightGBM − RF        +0.42   [+0.08, +0.75]   0.046

The 5×2cv test gives Holm-corrected p-values of 0.553, 0.245 and 0.002 for the same three pairs.

The “ten orders of magnitude” win over Logistic Regression is gone: p = 0.055 after correction. LightGBM’s edge over Random Forest survives (p = 0.046), and 5×2cv agrees.

Step 4: “Not significant” ≠ “the same”

A non-significant result might just mean the test lacked power. To claim the models are practically the same, you have to flip the hypotheses. That’s what an equivalence test does — TOST (two one-sided tests):

  • H₀: the difference is at least 1 pp in either direction
  • H₁: the difference is within ±1 pp
                    90% CI (pp)       TOST p   Equivalent?
RF − LogReg         [−0.27, +0.29]    2e-8     yes
LightGBM − LogReg   [+0.11, +0.74]    0.002    yes
LightGBM − RF       [+0.14, +0.70]    0.0004   yes

This is the most useful result in the whole analysis. We can confidently rule out that LightGBM is ≥ 1 pp better than either alternative.

And it’s not a contradiction that LightGBM vs Random Forest is both “significant” and “equivalent”. The difference is real, and it is small.

Step 5: The hold-out set, and a ranking that flips

The remaining 20% of the data (2,709 beans) was untouched until the very end. I fit each model once and ran two more tests:

  • McNemar’s test looks only at beans where the two models disagree. If neither model is better, a disagreement should go either way 50/50.
  • A paired bootstrap resamples the test set 10,000 times and recomputes the macro-F1 difference each time.
                 LogReg   RF       LightGBM
Hold-out F1      0.9302   0.9302   0.9286

LightGBM came last. None of the differences is significant (McNemar p between 0.28 and 1.0), and every bootstrap confidence interval contains zero.

That’s no paradox. A 2.7k-sample test set has a macro-F1 noise band of about ±0.6 pp, which is larger than the true gap of about 0.4 pp. If you choose models from a single train/test split, you’re partly choosing by coin flip.

Step 6: Look beyond the headline metric

I ran the corrected test on all 11 metrics as an exploratory check. One stood out — log loss, which measures the quality of the predicted probabilities (lower is better):

                 LogReg   RF       LightGBM
Log loss (CV)    0.214    0.275    0.340

All three pairwise log-loss differences are highly significant. The tree ensembles, especially boosting, are over-confident. If anything downstream uses the probabilities — thresholds, expected-cost decisions, ranking — that matters far more than 0.4 pp of F1.

A note on Hamming loss, which is often reported for this kind of task: for single-label multi-class problems it is identically 1 − accuracy (here 0.0753 vs an accuracy of 0.9247). Report it if you’re asked to, but don’t count it as separate evidence.

The verdict

                    Significant?   ≥ 1 pp?   Equivalent?
RF vs LogReg        no  (0.952)    no        yes
LightGBM vs LogReg  no  (0.055)    no        yes
LightGBM vs RF      yes (0.046)    no        yes

Ship the Logistic Regression. It is practically as accurate as the alternatives, has the best-calibrated probabilities, trains about 70× faster than the forest, and you can read its coefficients.

Five takeaways for your next model comparison

  1. Write the decision rule first: the metric, α, and the smallest difference you care about.
  2. Pair your comparisons. Use the same folds for every model, and test the differences.
  3. Never run a plain t-test on CV folds. Use the Nadeau–Bengio correction or 5×2cv.
  4. Separate “significant” from “meaningful”. Add an equivalence test, and correct for multiple comparisons.
  5. Look at calibration and cost, not just the headline score.
